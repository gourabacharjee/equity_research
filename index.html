📖 Module 1 · Beginner

What is Equity Research?

Imagine you're buying a house. Before paying lakhs or crores, you'd research the neighbourhood, check the construction quality, compare prices, and estimate future value. Equity research is exactly this — but for stocks (shares of companies).

💡 Simple Definition Equity Research is the process of analysing a company's financial health, industry position, and future prospects to determine whether its stock is a good investment at the current price. The output is an Equity Research Report with a recommendation: BUY, HOLD, or SELL.

Why Does Equity Research Exist?

Investors (mutual funds, pension funds, wealthy individuals) manage billions of rupees/dollars. They can't personally analyse every company. They rely on equity research analysts to provide well-researched, data-driven recommendations.

🔍 Real-World Analogy

Think of an equity research analyst as a "Stock Detective". Just like a detective gathers clues, interviews witnesses, and builds a case — an analyst reads financial statements, studies the industry, talks to management, and builds a case for why a stock should be bought or sold.

The Two Sides of Research

The equity research world has two major divisions:

📄 Module 2 · Beginner

Understanding Financial Statements

Financial statements are a company's official scorecards. Just like a student's report card tells you about their academic performance, financial statements tell you about a company's financial health. There are three core statements — and every equity analyst must master them.

📌 Where to Find Them In India: Annual Reports on BSE/NSE, or websites like Screener.in, MoneyControl.com. In the US: SEC EDGAR (sec.gov) for 10-K (annual) and 10-Q (quarterly) filings. Companies also publish them on their Investor Relations page.

The Income Statement — "The Report Card"

The Income Statement (also called Profit & Loss or P&L) shows how much money a company earned and spent over a period (usually a quarter or year). The bottom line tells you if the company made a profit or a loss.

Revenue − Expenses = Net Income (Profit/Loss)
The fundamental income statement equation

Key Line Items Explained

Line ItemWhat It MeansExample
Revenue (Sales) Total money earned from selling products/services A bakery sells ₹10,00,000 worth of cakes in a year
Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) Direct costs to make the product (raw materials, labour) Flour, sugar, bakers' wages = ₹4,00,000
Gross Profit Revenue minus COGS — profit before overhead ₹10,00,000 − ₹4,00,000 = ₹6,00,000
Operating Expenses (SG&A) Rent, marketing, admin salaries Shop rent + ads + manager salary = ₹3,00,000
Operating Income (EBIT) Profit from core business operations ₹6,00,000 − ₹3,00,000 = ₹3,00,000
Interest & Taxes Loan interest + government taxes Interest ₹50,000 + Tax ₹62,500 = ₹1,12,500
Net Income The "bottom line" — actual profit for shareholders ₹3,00,000 − ₹1,12,500 = ₹1,87,500
🧁 Bakery Example — Full Income Statement

Imagine you own "Sweet Dreams Bakery". Your annual Income Statement would look like:

Revenue: ₹10,00,000
(−) COGS: ₹4,00,000
= Gross Profit: ₹6,00,000 (60% margin — great!)
(−) Operating Expenses: ₹3,00,000
= EBIT: ₹3,00,000 (30% operating margin)
(−) Interest: ₹50,000
(−) Tax (25%): ₹62,500
= Net Income: ₹1,87,500 (18.75% net margin)

The Balance Sheet — "The Photograph"

While the Income Statement covers a period, the Balance Sheet is a snapshot of what the company owns and owes at a single point in time (like December 31, 2024).

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity
The accounting equation — must ALWAYS balance
🏠

Assets

What the company OWNS

  • Cash & bank balances
  • Inventory (unsold goods)
  • Accounts Receivable (money owed by customers)
  • Property, plant & equipment
  • Intangible assets (patents, brand)
💳

Liabilities

What the company OWES

  • Short-term loans
  • Accounts Payable (money owed to suppliers)
  • Long-term debt (bonds, bank loans)
  • Tax obligations
  • Employee benefits payable
👥

Shareholders' Equity

What belongs to OWNERS

  • Share capital (money raised from investors)
  • Retained earnings (accumulated profits)
  • Reserves
🧁 Bakery Example — Balance Sheet

Sweet Dreams Bakery's balance sheet on March 31, 2025:

ASSETS:
Cash: ₹2,00,000 | Inventory (flour, sugar): ₹1,00,000 | Oven & Equipment: ₹5,00,000 | Shop Deposit: ₹2,00,000
Total Assets = ₹10,00,000

LIABILITIES:
Supplier Bills Pending: ₹1,00,000 | Bank Loan: ₹3,00,000
Total Liabilities = ₹4,00,000

EQUITY:
Your Investment: ₹4,12,500 | Retained Earnings: ₹1,87,500
Total Equity = ₹6,00,000

✅ Check: Assets (₹10,00,000) = Liabilities (₹4,00,000) + Equity (₹6,00,000) ✓

The Cash Flow Statement — "The Bank Statement"

The most important statement many beginners overlook! A company can show a profit on the Income Statement but still run out of cash. The Cash Flow Statement tracks actual cash moving in and out.

⚠️ Why Cash Flow Matters "Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, cash flow is reality." A company can book revenue when it invoices a customer, even if the customer hasn't paid yet. The cash flow statement shows whether actual cash came in.

Three Sections of Cash Flow

⚙️

Operating Activities

Cash from core business — selling products, paying suppliers, paying salaries. This should be positive for a healthy company.

🏗️

Investing Activities

Cash spent on buying or selling long-term assets — equipment, property, acquisitions. Usually negative (spending money to grow).

🏦

Financing Activities

Cash from raising money (loans, issuing shares) or returning money (repaying debt, paying dividends).

🧁 Bakery Example — Cash Flow

Sweet Dreams Bakery's annual cash flow:

Operating: Received ₹9,50,000 from customers, Paid ₹7,00,000 for expenses
= Operating Cash Flow: +₹2,50,000 ✅ Healthy!

Investing: Bought new oven for ₹1,50,000
= Investing Cash Flow: −₹1,50,000

Financing: Repaid ₹50,000 of bank loan
= Financing Cash Flow: −₹50,000

Net Change in Cash: +₹50,000 (The bakery's bank balance grew!)

📐 Module 3 · Intermediate

Financial Ratios & Metrics

Financial ratios are shortcuts to understanding a company's health. Instead of reading through pages of financial statements, ratios let you quickly assess profitability, risk, and value using simple formulas. Think of them as a company's "vital signs" — like a doctor checking heart rate and blood pressure.

Valuation Ratios — "Is the stock cheap or expensive?"

These ratios help you determine if a stock is fairly priced relative to its earnings, assets, or growth.

Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio

P/E = Stock Price ÷ Earnings Per Share (EPS)

What it tells you: How much investors are willing to pay for each ₹1 of earnings.

Example: If a stock trades at ₹500 and EPS is ₹25, P/E = 20x. This means investors pay ₹20 for every ₹1 of profit.

Rule of thumb: A high P/E (>25x) suggests the market expects high growth. A low P/E (<15x) may mean the stock is undervalued — or the company has problems.

EV/EBITDA

EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA

What it tells you: How expensive a company is relative to its operating profitability, regardless of capital structure.

Why used: Unlike P/E, this works for companies with different debt levels because it removes the effects of interest, taxes, and depreciation.

Typical range: 8–15x for most industries. Tech companies may be higher.

Profitability Ratios — "How well does the company make money?"

Return on Equity (ROE)

ROE = Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100

What it tells you: How much profit the company generates with shareholders' money.

Example: Net Income ₹50 Cr, Equity ₹200 Cr → ROE = 25%. For every ₹100 invested by shareholders, the company earned ₹25.

Benchmark: ROE > 15% is generally considered good.

Net Profit Margin

Net Margin = Net Income ÷ Revenue × 100

What it tells you: What percentage of every rupee of revenue turns into actual profit.

Example: Revenue ₹1,000 Cr, Net Income ₹150 Cr → Margin = 15%. Out of every ₹100 earned, ₹15 is profit.

Varies by industry: IT services ~20%+, Retail ~3–5%, Banks ~15–25%.

Liquidity Ratios — "Can the company pay its short-term bills?"

Current Ratio

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

What it tells you: The company's ability to pay short-term obligations (due within 1 year).

Example: Current Assets ₹300 Cr, Current Liabilities ₹200 Cr → Ratio = 1.5. The company has ₹1.50 for every ₹1 it owes short-term.

Rule of thumb: > 1.0 is safe, > 2.0 is comfortable, < 1.0 is risky.

Quick Ratio (Acid Test)

Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities

What it tells you: Same as Current Ratio, but excludes inventory (because inventory may not sell quickly).

Why stricter: Inventory can be hard to convert to cash. If a company has too much unsold stock, the current ratio overstates liquidity.

Rule of thumb: > 1.0 is generally healthy.

Leverage Ratios — "How much debt is the company using?"

Debt-to-Equity (D/E) Ratio

D/E = Total Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity

What it tells you: How much the company relies on borrowed money vs. shareholders' money.

Example: Debt ₹400 Cr, Equity ₹500 Cr → D/E = 0.8. The company uses ₹0.80 of debt for every ₹1 of equity.

Rule of thumb: D/E < 1.0 is conservative, 1.0–2.0 is moderate, > 2.0 is aggressive.

Interest Coverage Ratio

Interest Coverage = EBIT ÷ Interest Expense

What it tells you: How easily the company can pay interest on its debt.

Example: EBIT ₹100 Cr, Interest ₹20 Cr → Coverage = 5x. The company earns 5 times its interest obligation.

Rule of thumb: > 3x is comfortable, < 1.5x is dangerous.

Efficiency Ratios — "How well does the company use its resources?"

Asset Turnover

Asset Turnover = Revenue ÷ Total Assets

What it tells you: How efficiently the company uses its assets to generate revenue.

Example: Revenue ₹500 Cr, Assets ₹250 Cr → Turnover = 2.0x. Each ₹1 of assets generates ₹2 of revenue.

Inventory Turnover

Inventory Turnover = COGS ÷ Average Inventory

What it tells you: How quickly inventory is sold and replaced.

Example: COGS ₹200 Cr, Avg Inventory ₹40 Cr → Turnover = 5x. The company sells through its entire inventory 5 times a year.

🌟 Golden Rule of Ratios Never look at a ratio in isolation. Always compare: (1) with the company's own history (is it improving?), (2) with industry peers (is it better than competitors?), and (3) with industry benchmarks.
💎 Module 4 · Intermediate-Advanced

Valuation Methods

Valuation is the heart of equity research. It answers the most important question: "What is this company actually worth?" If the market price is below your estimated value, the stock may be undervalued (a potential BUY). If above, it may be overvalued (a potential SELL).

🎯 Two Schools of Thought Intrinsic Valuation (DCF): "What is the company worth based on future cash flows?"
Relative Valuation (Comps): "What is the company worth compared to similar companies?"
Most analysts use BOTH methods to create a valuation range.

Method 1: Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis

The DCF is considered the "gold standard" of valuation. The core idea is simple: a company is worth the total cash it will generate in the future, discounted back to today's value.

💡 The Core Concept — Time Value of Money

Would you rather have ₹100 today or ₹100 one year from now? Obviously today — because you could invest that ₹100 and earn interest. This means ₹100 tomorrow is worth less than ₹100 today. DCF "discounts" future cash flows to find what they're worth right now.

Step-by-Step DCF Walkthrough

1

Project Free Cash Flow (FCF)

Estimate the company's Free Cash Flow for the next 5–10 years. FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures. This is the cash available to all investors after the company has invested in its business.

2

Calculate the Discount Rate (WACC)

Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) represents the minimum return investors expect. It blends the cost of equity (what shareholders expect) and cost of debt (interest rate on loans), weighted by how much of each the company uses. Typical WACC: 8–15%.

3

Estimate Terminal Value

Companies don't stop after 10 years. Terminal Value estimates the company's worth beyond your forecast period. Common methods: (a) Gordon Growth Model: FCF × (1 + growth rate) ÷ (WACC − growth rate), or (b) Exit Multiple: Final year EBITDA × industry EV/EBITDA multiple.

4

Discount Everything to Present Value

Divide each year's FCF by (1 + WACC)^n where n is the year number. Sum them all up with the discounted terminal value. This gives you the Enterprise Value (EV).

5

Calculate Equity Value per Share

Equity Value = Enterprise Value − Net Debt (Total Debt − Cash). Then divide by total shares outstanding to get the Fair Value per Share. Compare this to the current stock price!

🔢 Simplified DCF Example — TechVista Corp

Let's value a hypothetical company, TechVista Corp:

Assumptions: FCF Year 1: ₹100 Cr, growing at 12% for 5 years, WACC: 11%, Terminal Growth: 4%

Year-by-Year FCF Projection:
Year 1: ₹100 Cr → PV = ₹100 ÷ 1.11 = ₹90.1 Cr
Year 2: ₹112 Cr → PV = ₹112 ÷ 1.11² = ₹90.9 Cr
Year 3: ₹125.4 Cr → PV = ₹125.4 ÷ 1.11³ = ₹91.7 Cr
Year 4: ₹140.5 Cr → PV = ₹140.5 ÷ 1.11⁴ = ₹92.5 Cr
Year 5: ₹157.4 Cr → PV = ₹157.4 ÷ 1.11⁵ = ₹93.3 Cr

Sum of Discounted FCFs = ₹458.5 Cr

Terminal Value = ₹157.4 × (1.04) ÷ (0.11 − 0.04) = ₹2,339 Cr
PV of Terminal Value = ₹2,339 ÷ 1.11⁵ = ₹1,387 Cr

Enterprise Value = ₹458.5 + ₹1,387 = ₹1,845.5 Cr
(−) Net Debt: ₹200 Cr
Equity Value = ₹1,645.5 Cr
Shares Outstanding: 10 Cr
Fair Value per Share = ₹164.55

If the stock currently trades at ₹130, it appears undervalued — potential BUY!

Method 2: Comparable Company Analysis ("Comps")

Comps is like buying a flat — you check what similar flats in the same area sold for. You compare the company to similar publicly traded companies using valuation multiples.

1

Select a Peer Group

Find 5–10 similar companies based on: same industry, similar size (revenue), similar geography, similar growth rates and margins.

2

Calculate Valuation Multiples for Each Peer

Common multiples: P/E, EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue, P/B (Price-to-Book). Gather these from financial databases or calculate manually.

3

Find the Median/Mean

Calculate the median (preferred — less affected by outliers) or mean of each multiple across the peer group.

4

Apply to Your Target Company

Multiply the median multiple by your company's relevant metric. Example: If median P/E of peers is 18x and your company's EPS is ₹25, implied price = 18 × ₹25 = ₹450.

📋 Module 5 · Advanced

Building an Equity Research Report

An equity research report is the final product of all your analysis. It's the document you hand to an investor (or your client/portfolio manager) with your recommendation. A well-structured report demonstrates credibility, thoroughness, and clear thinking.

📌 Remember The best reports are not the longest — they're the clearest. An investor should understand your thesis within the first page. Think of it as telling a compelling "story" backed by data.

The 8 Essential Sections

1️⃣ Front Page / Cover ▼

The cover page is your report's "first impression". It should include:

  • Company Name & Ticker — e.g., "TechVista Corp (NSE: TECHVISTA)"
  • Current Market Price (CMP) — The stock's current trading price
  • Target Price — Your estimated fair value
  • Recommendation — BUY / HOLD / SELL with clear visual indicator
  • Upside/Downside % — How much the stock could gain or lose
  • Key Metrics Snapshot — Market Cap, P/E, Revenue, Sector
  • Date of Report & Analyst Name
  • Investment Horizon — Usually 12 months
2️⃣ Executive Summary & Investment Thesis ▼

The most important section — often the only part busy investors read fully. In 4–6 bullet points, explain:

  • Your Thesis: WHY you recommend this stock (the core argument)
  • Key Catalysts: What specific events will drive the price? (new product launch, market expansion, margin improvement)
  • Valuation Summary: Brief mention of your DCF/Comps result
  • Key Risks: Top 2–3 risks an investor must be aware of
🌟 Pro Tip Your thesis must answer: "What does the market NOT see, that I see?" Simply restating publicly known information adds no value. Your unique insight is what makes a report valuable.
3️⃣ Company Overview ▼
  • Business Description: What does the company do? Products/services overview
  • Business Model: How does it make money? Revenue breakdown by segment/geography
  • Management: Who leads the company? Track record of key executives
  • History & Milestones: Key events that shaped the company
  • Competitive Advantages (Moat): What makes this company hard to compete with?
4️⃣ Industry & Competitive Analysis ▼
  • Industry Size & Growth: Total Addressable Market (TAM), growth rate (CAGR)
  • Industry Trends: Technology shifts, regulatory changes, consumer behaviour
  • Competitive Landscape: Major players, market share data
  • Porter's Five Forces: Threat of new entrants, supplier power, buyer power, substitutes, rivalry
  • SWOT Analysis: Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats
5️⃣ Financial Analysis & Forecasts ▼
  • Historical Financials (3–5 years): Revenue, EBITDA, Net Income, FCF trends
  • Key Ratios Analysis: Profitability, leverage, liquidity trends
  • Revenue Model: Segment-wise revenue breakdown and projections
  • Projected Financials (3–5 years): Your forecast of future Income Statement, Balance Sheet, Cash Flow
  • Key Assumptions: Clearly state your assumptions (growth rate, margins, capex) so readers can evaluate your model
6️⃣ Valuation ▼
  • DCF Analysis: Show your DCF model inputs and output
  • Comparable Company Analysis: Peer comparison table with multiples
  • Sensitivity Analysis: Table showing how target price changes with different WACC/growth assumptions
  • Target Price Derivation: How you arrived at your final target price (often a blend of DCF and Comps)
  • Upside/Downside Scenario: Bull case, base case, bear case prices
7️⃣ Investment Risks ▼

A credible report always discusses risks honestly. Types to cover:

  • Business/Execution Risk: Can management actually deliver on their plans?
  • Industry/Competition Risk: Could competitors erode market share?
  • Regulatory Risk: Government policy changes that could impact the business
  • Macroeconomic Risk: Recession, interest rate hikes, currency fluctuations
  • Valuation Risk: Key assumptions in your model that could be wrong
8️⃣ Disclosures & Disclaimers ▼

This is a legally required section. It protects both you and the reader.

  • Rating Definitions: What "BUY", "HOLD", "SELL" specifically mean (e.g., BUY = expected upside > 15%)
  • Conflict of Interest: Does the analyst or firm own shares? Does the firm have a banking relationship?
  • Data Sources: Where did you get your data? (Company filings, Bloomberg, Reuters, etc.)
  • Standard Disclaimer: "This report is for informational purposes only and does not constitute investment advice..."
🌟 Professional Tips for Report Writing 1. Lead with your conclusion — don't make readers hunt for your recommendation.
2. Use charts and tables — a well-placed chart is worth 500 words.
3. Be concise — target 15–25 pages for an initiating report, 2–5 pages for updates.
4. Show your work — display assumptions so readers can challenge your thinking.
5. Be honest about risks — one-sided bullish reports lose credibility fast.
🤖 Module 6 · Advanced

Using AI for Equity Research

Artificial Intelligence is transforming equity research. AI won't replace analysts, but analysts who use AI will replace those who don't. The key philosophy is "Human-in-the-Loop" — AI handles the heavy lifting of data processing, while you provide judgment, creativity, and the final investment call.

⚠️ Critical Rule AI can "hallucinate" — generate plausible-sounding but incorrect information. NEVER publish AI-generated financial data without verifying it against primary sources (annual reports, SEC filings). Treat every AI output as a first draft that requires your review.

Best Practices for Using AI in Research

✅

DO

  • Always provide source documents (don't rely on AI's memory)
  • Verify every number against the original source
  • Use AI for the "first 70%" and add your insight for the "last 30%"
  • Start small — automate one bottleneck at a time
  • Use enterprise AI tools for sensitive/proprietary data

Glossary & Resources

Quick reference for key terms used throughout this course, plus curated resources for deeper learning.

Key Terms Dictionary

🔍
Alpha
Excess returns generated above the benchmark index. If the Nifty returns 12% and your portfolio returns 18%, your alpha is 6%.
Bear Market
A market decline of 20% or more from recent highs. Opposite of a bull market.
Beta
A measure of a stock's volatility compared to the market. Beta of 1.5 means the stock is 50% more volatile than the market.
Bull Market
A period of rising stock prices, typically 20%+ from a recent low.
CAGR
Compound Annual Growth Rate — the average annual growth rate over a specified time period. Formula: (End/Start)^(1/n) − 1.
Capex
Capital Expenditure — money spent by a company to acquire, upgrade, or maintain physical assets (property, plants, equipment).
CMP
Current Market Price — the price at which a stock is currently trading.
COGS
Cost of Goods Sold — the direct costs of producing the goods a company sells (raw materials, direct labour).
DCF
Discounted Cash Flow — a valuation method that estimates the value of an investment based on its expected future cash flows, discounted to present value.
D/E Ratio
Debt-to-Equity Ratio — measures a company's financial leverage. Total Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity.
Dividend Yield
Annual dividend per share ÷ Stock Price × 100. Shows what percentage return you get from dividends alone.
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation & Amortization — a measure of operating profitability that removes the effects of financing and accounting decisions.
Enterprise Value (EV)
Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash. Represents the total value of a company, accounting for both equity and debt holders.
EPS
Earnings Per Share — Net Income ÷ Total Shares Outstanding. Shows how much profit each share earns.
FCF
Free Cash Flow — Operating Cash Flow minus Capital Expenditures. The cash available to investors after the company reinvests in itself.
FII
Foreign Institutional Investor — overseas institutional entities (banks, funds) investing in a country's securities market.
Gross Margin
(Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue × 100. Shows profitability after accounting for direct production costs.
Market Cap
Market Capitalization = Stock Price × Total Shares Outstanding. Represents the total market value of a company's equity.
MNPI
Material Non-Public Information — significant info about a company not yet available to the public. Trading on MNPI is illegal (insider trading).
Moat
A company's competitive advantage that protects it from competitors — like a castle's moat. Examples: brand, patents, network effects, cost advantages.
Net Income
The "bottom line" — total profit after all expenses, interest, and taxes are deducted from revenue.
P/B Ratio
Price-to-Book Ratio — Stock Price ÷ Book Value per Share. Compares market price to the company's net asset value.
P/E Ratio
Price-to-Earnings Ratio — Stock Price ÷ EPS. Shows how much investors pay for ₹1 of earnings.
Peer Group
A set of comparable companies used for relative valuation. Selected based on industry, size, growth, and geography.
ROA
Return on Assets — Net Income ÷ Total Assets × 100. Measures how efficiently a company uses its assets to generate profit.
ROE
Return on Equity — Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100. Measures how efficiently a company generates profit from shareholders' money.
ROIC
Return on Invested Capital — NOPAT ÷ Invested Capital × 100. Measures how well a company generates returns from all invested capital.
Sensitivity Analysis
A "what-if" analysis showing how changes in key assumptions (growth rate, WACC) affect the valuation outcome.
TAM
Total Addressable Market — the total revenue opportunity available for a product or service.
Terminal Value
In DCF, the estimated value of a company beyond the explicit forecast period, representing cash flows into perpetuity.
WACC
Weighted Average Cost of Capital — the blended cost of a company's debt and equity financing. Used as the discount rate in DCF.
YoY
Year-over-Year — comparing a metric to the same period in the previous year.
